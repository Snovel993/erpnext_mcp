# v0.75.0 — the receipt says more than the merchant line

Seven columns, one taught register, and a cascade that stops guessing at a
string it cannot read.

---

## The gap

v0.68.0 gave this app `normalize_merchant`: strip punctuation and legal-form
words from both sides, compare what is left, and `WILBUR ELLIS CO` lands on
`Wilbur-Ellis Company LLC` at a confidence a person can accept. No ML, auditable
by reading it, and right about most of a farm's vendors.

It is structurally incapable of being right about the rest.

```
merchant line:   SIATAPING
the vendor:      Sawyer's Ace Hardware
```

There is no string algorithm that gets from the first to the second, because the
letters are not there — it is a point-of-sale terminal's own abbreviation of a
franchise name, and the same till will print it every week for years. Every
receipt from that shop arrives unresolved, a bookkeeper codes each one by hand,
and the next one arrives unresolved again. The app learned nothing from being
told.

**The evidence was on the paper the whole time.** The same slip prints a domain,
a phone number, a store number and the last four digits of the card. Each of
those is a *harder* identifier than the name: a domain is registered to one
company, a phone number rings in one building, a card's last four picks one of
two trucks out of a fuel account. None of them was captured, so the weakest
signal on the receipt was the only one being used.

**And the bank had the same problem downstream.** `auto_match_receipts` reports
two $47.83 fuel slips on one day at one station as *contested* and hands them to
a person, because amount, date and vendor cannot tell them apart. Two trucks,
two drivers — and two cards, whose last four the bank prints in the memo line.

## What shipped

### Seven Custom Fields on Expense Receipt

Four are what the scanner read besides the name; three are what reading it
produced.

| Column | What it holds |
|---|---|
| `card_last_four` | the last four digits of the card, as printed |
| `merchant_phone` | the phone number on the slip |
| `merchant_url` | the domain on the slip |
| `store_number` | the store or location number |
| `resolved_merchant` | what the merchant **actually is** |
| `resolution_method` | `OCR` / `URL` / `Phone` / `Alias` / `LLM` / `Manual` |
| `resolution_confidence` | 0–1 |

`merchant` and `resolved_merchant` are separate columns for ever. The first is
what the scanner read; the second is what it means. Overwriting the reading with
the conclusion would delete the only evidence anybody could ever use to find out
the conclusion was wrong.

They are **Custom Fields on this app's own doctype**, which looks odd until you
ask what the alternative costs. Expense Receipt is a register sites have been
capturing into since v0.31.0; putting seven more columns in the shipped JSON
makes them mandatory schema for a bench that will never enable receipt
intelligence, and makes the feature un-droppable — a Custom Field is removable in
the Desk by the operator who decided they did not want it, and a JSON field is
removable only by a release. Installed at `bench migrate` and lazily on first
use, so a bench that pulled the code without running the installer resolves a
merchant the first time somebody captures a receipt.

**A site that will not take them keeps everything.** The cascade still runs and
still reports its answer; only the storing of it is skipped, and
`intelligence_fields_installed: false` says so rather than pretending.

### The `Merchant Alias` register, taught by work somebody was already doing

Setting a `supplier` with `update_expense_receipt` now records the mapping.
Coding one `SIATAPING` slip to Sawyer's Ace Hardware teaches every future one —
and nothing is asked of the bookkeeper, who was never shown a form about
aliases. The register grows out of the correcting they were doing anyway.

**The docname is the normalised alias.** "one alias resolves to exactly one
Supplier" is the invariant the whole cascade depends on: a first step that could
return two answers is not a first step. Making the key the primary key means the
database holds that invariant rather than a validation somebody can forget to
run, and it makes `Valley Co-op #14` and `VALLEY CO-OP 14` one row instead of two
that will eventually disagree.

`alias_learned.action` names what happened — `created`, `repointed`, `unchanged`
or `skipped` — because "did that do anything" is the caller's next question. A
re-point is **not** a mistake: a later human decision beats an earlier one, and
the `match_count` is kept rather than reset, so correcting a long-standing alias
does not make it look brand new. A merchant that already normalises to the
Supplier's own name is `skipped`: name matching finds it, and a row would be one
fact stored twice.

**Learning never fails the update.** It is a side effect of a write that has
already succeeded, and an alias register that refuses a row must not turn a
completed supplier correction into an error somebody has to interpret.

### The five-step cascade

| # | Step | What it is | Confidence |
|---|------|-----------|-----------|
| 1 | `Alias` | a person's own earlier link for this exact spelling, replayed | **1.0** taught by hand, 0.95 taught by this app or a model |
| 2 | `URL` | a domain, registered to one company | 0.90 (0.85 from other coded receipts) |
| 3 | `Phone` | a number, ringing in one building | 0.88 (0.83 from other coded receipts) |
| 4 | `OCR` | the v0.68.0 name similarity, unchanged | whatever it scores |
| 5 | `LLM` | the raw text read as prose | the caller's own |

Step 1 is the only thing in this app that reaches 1.0, and the distinction is
real rather than rhetorical: every other step is an algorithm's opinion, and a
hand-taught alias is a person's own decision handed back to them. An alias this
app taught itself, or that a model taught it, replays at the same 0.95 ceiling
everything else lives under — it was a guess when it was written, and repetition
does not make it a fact.

Steps 2 and 3 ask the Supplier register first and the **receipt corpus** second:
receipts somebody already coded by hand are the answer to "whose phone number is
this", they are already on the site, and a second register holding the same fact
would be a second place for it to be wrong. Two coded receipts disagreeing about
one number resolve to *neither* — with two answers there is no answer, and
inventing one is worse than saying so.

**Every step is reported, including the ones that found nothing.** `steps[]`
carries `tried`, `matched` and a sentence per step, because "why didn't the URL
match" is the interesting question on the day a farm's receipts stop resolving,
and a cascade that only reported its winner would make it unanswerable.

**Nothing is still inferred, with exactly one exception.** An alias hit sets the
`supplier` link — not an inference, a replay — and says so under
`supplier_resolved_by`. A domain match, a phone match, a name score and a
caller's own LLM answer all stop at `resolved_merchant` and leave the link to a
person, which is the rule `submit_expense_receipt` has had since v0.31.0.

### Four anchored patterns, and the refusals that make them worth having

Send `ocr_raw_text` alone and the four signals are read off it. An argument
always beats the text — the phone had the full-resolution image and this app has
a text blob — and `signals_from_raw_text[]` names which were read rather than
sent, because those are worth different amounts of trust when one turns out
wrong.

The refusals are the half that matters:

- **A bare four-digit run is never read as a card.** A receipt is full of them —
  a time, a total in cents, an item code. A fingerprint that matched a clock
  would manufacture confidence out of a coincidence.
- **A full card number is refused, not truncated.** Storing the fingerprint and
  silently dropping the rest would hide that a PAN was ever sent.
- **A payment-processor, survey or social domain is never the merchant.** It
  identifies the till's supplier, and it appears on receipts from hundreds of
  unrelated businesses.

### The card fingerprint, in the bank bridge

Where a receipt's `card_last_four` matches the digits in a bank memo line
**within a day**, the proposal comes back with `card_fingerprint: true`, gains
`+0.15` toward the 0.95 ceiling, and **outranks a higher-scoring receipt with no
fingerprint**. The bank naming the physical card that was swiped is better
evidence about *which slip this is* than any margin in a similarity number — so
the two-trucks case that used to be `contested` is now two proposals, each with
its reason on it.

Three conditions, all required. The memo line has to name the digits **as a
card** — masked, or introduced by a word that means card — because a bare
four-digit run in a memo is as likely a terminal id. And the window is one day,
not seven: the wide window is for *finding* a match, this is for *confirming*
one, and a same-card same-amount charge a week later is next week's fill-up at
the same pump.

**It is a bonus, not a fourth weight.** The three weights are a partition of one
unit of evidence about a purchase; folding the card into them would have meant
taking weight away from the amount, which is the signal nearly impossible to
coincide by accident. A receipt with no card scores **exactly** what it scored
before this release.

## What this release deliberately does not do

**It does not call a model.** Step 5 prepares the question — the signals off the
paper, the brand-naming lines from the raw text, the candidate Suppliers — and
returns it as `llm_context` for a caller that has a model, exactly as
`validate_document_extraction` has done since v0.69.0. A step that quietly
dialled an API would put a network call, a key and a bill inside a `bench
migrate`, and would make a capture at a fuel pump fail for a reason nobody
standing there could diagnose. The answer comes back through
`resolved_merchant`.

**It adds no tools.** Every capability here lands on tools that already exist —
`submit_expense_receipt`, `update_expense_receipt`, `normalize_merchant`,
`list_merchant_aliases`, `auto_match_receipts` — so the catalogue is the same
size it was, and an operator has no new switch to reason about.

**It does not scope the alias register by company.** A Supplier is already
site-wide in ERPNext, and an alias is a fact about a shop's receipt printer, not
about anybody's operation — it names no amount, no date and no employee. A
company column would also break the one invariant the register rests on, by
making the alias key non-unique. `test_permissions.py` carries the argument.

**The resolution triple is not on the mobile endpoint.**
`create_expense_receipt` forwards the four capture signals, because the phone is
where they are read — and deliberately not `resolved_merchant` /
`resolution_method` / `resolution_confidence`, because those short-circuit the
whole cascade and would let a bad on-device guess overrule a mapping a
bookkeeper taught by hand. The phone reports what it READ; deciding what that
means stays on this side.

## Tests

`tests_standalone/test_receipt_intelligence.py`, 80 tests in nine classes. The
two that matter most:

- `test_a_domain_match_never_sets_the_supplier_link` — everything here is
  machinery for a better *suggestion*, and a refactor that let a 0.90 domain
  match set a link would be invisible in every output payload while putting
  fabricated vendor attributions across a season of books.
- `test_the_cascade_never_calls_a_model` — asserted by breaking `urlopen`
  rather than assumed.

Full suite: 8324 tests, green.
