# v0.19.4 — what the shift was actually like

**Released 2026-08-03. The Open-Meteo service, the fifteen-minute sweep, the
Weather Settings doctype, threshold detection, historical backfill and five new
tools.**

v0.19.3 shipped `Farm Shift Weather Reading` with nothing writing to it, and said
so in the doctype's own description: the shape was fixed first so that wiring a
fetch would mean writing a service rather than migrating a schema under live
compliance records. This is the fetch.

It matters more than an integration usually does, because of what it replaces. A
foreman's logged water break says what was **done**; nothing on the shift said
what it was done **about**. OAR 437-004-1131 is a rule about conditions — it
engages at a heat index of 80 °F and adds obligations at 90 — so until now the
only way a temperature reached a compliance record was somebody typing it in,
usually in the evening, usually from memory. "About ninety-five" is precisely the
kind of figure an investigator discounts.

After this release nobody types a temperature at all. A cron walks every open
shift every fifteen minutes, asks Open-Meteo what the conditions are at that
shift's own coordinates, and appends an immutable reading. The heat record's
maxima compute off those readings. Each compliance event carries the conditions
at its own instant. And a shift that ran before any of this existed can be
reconstructed from the archive, because Open-Meteo still knows what the weather
was that day.

Suite: 3,734 → **3,835 passing**. Tool surface: 224 → **229** (102 read, 127
mutating).

---

## 1. The line this release draws

> The sweep surfaces the condition. The foreman decides whether it is a record.

Everything else here is engineering. This sentence is the design.

A weather reading is a fact about a place and a moment. "This reading is at or
above the threshold in Weather Settings" is also a fact — it is arithmetic over a
stored number, it needs nobody's judgement, and it is written automatically as a
`Threshold Crossed` compliance event on the shift's own timeline.

**A Heat Exposure Event is not written here and will not be.** That record says
which crew was exposed, whether water was provided at the required rate, whether
the rest cycle was actually *taken* rather than merely offered, whether anybody
showed signs of heat illness and what was done about it — and it carries the
supervisor's signature under all of it. Five judgements by the person who was
standing in the block. A machine that filed one from a temperature reading would
be producing an attestation with nobody behind it, in the exact place where
having nobody behind it is the failure.

So the sweep logs the observation, the result of every weather tool says in
plain words that `create_heat_exposure_event` is where a person writes the
record, and the tests assert that no Heat Exposure Event exists after a 99 °F
sweep. The refusal is the feature.

## 2. The service

`erpnext_mcp/services/weather.py`, in a new `services/` package whose whole
premise is one property: **a module in here talks to somebody else's server**,
which is a different kind of code with a different set of ways to fail.

It is a port in **shape** rather than in code from `farm_app/app/utils/weather.py`.
That module is the Flask side's agronomy surface — soil temperature at four
depths, chill hours, growing degree days, Penman-Monteith evapotranspiration,
field capacity estimated from post-rain plateaus. Not one of those functions is
what a shift needs and none is reused. What carried over is the idiom: one
`requests.get` with an explicit timeout, `raise_for_status`, a normalised dict
out, an error path that returns rather than throws, and Open-Meteo's two hosts as
the source of truth because it is free, needs no API key and answers for any
coordinate on earth.

### The heat index is computed, not read

Open-Meteo returns `apparent_temperature`. It is not the same number. Apparent
temperature folds in wind and solar radiation and is a wind-chill figure in
winter; the NWS heat index is temperature and relative humidity, and it is what
-1131 turns on.

At **88 °F and 70 % relative humidity** the two disagree by about ten degrees,
and the direction is the one that matters: the heat index is **100.2 °F** and the
rule engages, while apparent temperature says 89 and a shift documented against
it would look compliant. So `heat_index_f` is the Rothfusz regression the NWS
publishes, computed from the temperature and humidity the API *did* return, with
both stored beside it so a disputed index can be recomputed from the observation.
Where a reading comes back with no humidity, `apparent_temperature` is the
fallback and the row's `source` still says where the whole thing came from.

That 88/70 → 100 example is not invented for these notes. It is the worked
example in the `Farm Shift Weather Reading` doctype's own field description,
written in v0.19.3, and it is now a test.

### Every failure is a missing reading and nothing worse

Open-Meteo asks nobody for an API key, which is a reason to be **more** careful
with it rather than less: the only thing between this app and a rate limit is the
cache and the backoff.

- **429 or 5xx** → exponential backoff for that coordinate, doubling each time,
  capped at an hour. During it, no request goes out for that place at all. A
  success clears it, because continuing to refuse would be the app rate-limiting
  itself.
- **Timeout, connection error, malformed body, or a 200 carrying an HTML login
  page** from a captive portal → `None`.
- **A payload missing the block it promised** → `None`.
- **Nothing raises.** The sweep runs on a cron on somebody's bench beside their
  real work. A shift with a gap in its timeline is a worse record than one
  without a gap and an infinitely better outcome than a scheduler that stopped.

The cache is a plain module-level dict keyed by coordinates rounded to four
decimals — about eleven metres, which is the same block, and finer than the
distinction is worth since Open-Meteo models a grid square measured in
kilometres. Two shifts on adjacent rows of one orchard share a request. It is per
worker process and does not survive a restart, which is the right trade: a cold
cache costs one extra request per place, and the alternative is a new failure
mode in the path whose entire job is not to have any.

### Resolving a place

`farm_location_gps` is free text on purpose, so three spellings arrive and all
three are accepted: `45.52,-122.68`, `45.52, -122.68`, and
`Latitude 45.52, longitude -122.68` — the spelling the Farm Shift doctype itself
documents. Coordinates are parsed here and never leave the site.

A **place name** costs one geocoding request and is then cached, including
negatively: a name the geocoder does not know will not become known inside the
cache window, and asking again every tick is how a free service stops answering.

A transposed pair is **refused rather than clamped**. `-122.68,45.52` is a real
place in the Yellow Sea, and fetching weather for it and filing the answer
against an Oregon shift would be a fabricated record that looks exactly like a
true one.

**Ambiguity is resolved by taking the first match and saying so.** The geocoder
is asked for one result and returns the most populous place with that name, which
is right for "Milton-Freewater, OR" and wrong for a block somebody called "Home".
There is no way to be certain from a string, so the honest fix is transparency:
`fetch_weather_now` and `get_weather_settings` report the coordinates a name
resolved to, so a foreman who sees Ohio can put the numbers on the shift instead.

## 3. Weather Settings

A second Single, separate from `ERPNext MCP Settings` deliberately: that document
is the MCP endpoint's control panel, and this is an operational configuration a
farm manager should be able to open without being handed the tool switches and
the bearer token.

| Field | Default | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `enabled` | on | The kill switch for the whole surface. Off: the sweep returns without a query, both mutating tools refuse, and this site makes no outbound request to anybody. |
| `fetch_interval_minutes` | 15 | How old a shift's newest reading must be before the sweep fetches another. |
| `cache_ttl_seconds` | 300 | How long one place's answer is reused. |
| `http_timeout_seconds` | 10 | How long one call may take. |
| `heat_threshold_temp_f` | 80.0 | Air temperature a crossing fires at. |
| `heat_threshold_heat_index_f` | 80.0 | Heat index a crossing fires at — the number the rule actually turns on. |
| `wind_threshold_mph_spray_block` | 15.0 | Wind a crossing fires at, **on Spray shifts only**. |
| `open_meteo_base_url_current` / `_archive` / `_geocoding` | Open-Meteo's three hosts | Configurable, for a self-hosted instance or a mirror inside a closed network. |
| `per_company_overrides` | empty | Per-entity thresholds. |

**The cron is the ceiling and `fetch_interval_minutes` is the floor.** A Frappe
cron expression is a static string in `hooks.py` and cannot be rewritten from a
form, so the sweep runs every fifteen minutes on every site — and the setting is
honoured by *skipping* a shift whose newest reading is younger than it. Raising it
to 60 gets one reading an hour, which is the change operations actually ask for;
lowering it below 15 changes nothing. The field description says so on the form.

**Every override column is nullable and null means the parent.** A row that exists
to lower one entity's wind limit must leave its heat limits alone — a zero heat
threshold would make every reading on that entity a crossing, which is the same as
making none of them mean anything.

The controller refuses four things at save time rather than reporting them from a
job nobody reads:

1. **A non-positive timeout or TTL.** A timeout of zero is not "no timeout" to
   `requests`; it is a connection that fails immediately, every time, silently.
2. **A base URL that is not `http(s)`.** The service refuses to fetch one anyway,
   but a URL refused at fetch time is a timeline that quietly stops filling, and a
   URL refused at save time is a sentence on the operator's screen while they
   still remember what they meant to type.
3. **A negative threshold.** A minus key struck by accident makes *every* reading
   a crossing.
4. **Two override rows for one company.** `thresholds_for` reads the first match,
   so a second row makes that entity's threshold depend on grid order — which is a
   threshold nobody can defend under audit, because the number on the form and the
   number the sweep used need not be the same one.

`Weather Settings` defaults are seeded on install and on every migrate, by the
same function that seeds the MCP settings — the eighth install job. Idempotent, and
it never overwrites a choice including a deliberate "off". **Not a Frappe
`fixtures` entry**, which `test_hooks.py` forbids by name: a fixture is imported
with no ability to skip what a site already has, so an operator who lowered their
heat threshold to 75 °F would get it silently raised back on the next upgrade, and
the first anybody would know is a hot afternoon that logged nothing.

## 4. The fifth scheduled job

```python
scheduler_events = {
    "cron": {"*/15 * * * *": ["erpnext_mcp.services.weather.sweep_open_shifts"]},
    ...
}
```

The first `cron` entry this app has ever declared, and the fifth scheduled job.
`hooks.py` states the count in its docstring and `test_hooks.py` asserts the
mapping exactly, so a sixth cannot arrive without somebody arguing for it.

Fifteen minutes rather than `hourly` because of the rule it serves: -1131 asks
what the conditions were across an exposure period, and nine readings on a
nine-hour shift is a sketch where thirty-six is a timeline.

`sweep_open_shifts` filters on **`end_datetime` rather than on `status`**. The two
agree on every site where nothing has gone wrong, and `status` is a stored column
recomputed on save — so a shift written by an import that set the column directly
could read Closed while still being worked. The absence of an end time is the
fact; the status is a summary of it, and the sweep reads the fact.

**It is safe to run at any cadence, for three independent reasons** rather than
the alert sweep's one: the interval check skips a shift just read, the cache
answers the request that would have gone out, and `append_readings` refuses a
second row for a minute already on the timeline. One shift's failure is one
shift's failure — a block with nonsense coordinates does not stop the other twelve
crews being documented today.

## 5. Threshold crossings

When a live reading lands, it is evaluated against that shift's company's
thresholds.

**One event per shift, not one per reading.** A shift that ran nine hours above
eighty degrees would otherwise collect thirty-six identical rows, and a timeline
of thirty-six identical rows is one nobody reads — which would bury the water
breaks and the observations that are the evidence somebody actually needs to find.

The event carries **no `logged_by`**. It is a Link to Employee and nobody logged
this; the sweep did. Naming the foreman would put their identity against an
observation they did not make, on the one doctype whose entire value is that it
says who did what.

For wind: a crossing fires only on a **Spray** shift. Fifteen miles an hour during
a prune is weather; during an application it is a decision somebody has to make,
and nearly every pesticide label sets a maximum. (v0.22.5's CCF will be able to
auto-create a "suspend spray" task off the same signal. Today it logs the event.)

An existing Heat Exposure Event's `threshold_crossed_at` is moved **earlier** by a
reading that proves an earlier crossing, and never later. The obligations run from
the *first* moment the shift crossed, so overwriting with the latest would shorten
the documented exposure period every fifteen minutes — which is the direction that
flatters the operation and the direction an investigator would be right to
disbelieve. A **submitted** record is left alone entirely: it is signed, and an
automated edit to a signed attestation is exactly what signatures exist to prevent.

## 6. Three things that now fill themselves in

And in all three, **manual entry wins**. An on-site reading at the block beats a
modelled figure for a grid square measured in kilometres, and the foreman who
wrote 103 °F on a record they are about to sign is asserting something they saw.
The computed value fills a **blank**; it never corrects an answer.

**Compliance event snapshots.** `weather_snapshot_temp_f` and
`weather_snapshot_heat_index_f` come from the reading current at the event's own
instant — the last one *at or before* it, within half an hour. **Earlier beats
later**: the reading that was current at 09:15 is the conditions the foreman was
standing in when they called the break, not one taken five minutes afterwards. A
later reading is used only when there is nothing before, which is the ordinary case
for an event logged in the first minutes of a shift. Past thirty minutes nothing is
copied at all — a temperature from an hour away is not evidence about this moment,
and a blank column is honest where a stale one is not.

**Heat Exposure Event maxima.** `max_temp_f` and `max_heat_index_f` are the peaks
of the shift's timeline. `threshold_crossed_at` is the **earliest** crossing and
not the hottest moment, read against that company's own thresholds.

**Where these hooks live.** Both are in the **controllers of this app's own
doctypes**, not in `doc_events`. `hooks.py` promises this app installs no document
hooks and `test_hooks.py` forbids the key by name — because a `doc_events` entry is
how an app changes the behaviour of a doctype it does not own. `Farm Shift` and
`Heat Exposure Event` are this app's, so the rules belong in their controllers,
where they also run on a Desk edit.

## 7. Historical backfill

Every site that installs v0.19.4 has a season of shifts behind it with an empty
weather table. **A shift with no timeline is not a shift that was compliant or
non-compliant — it is a shift nobody can say anything about**, and Open-Meteo still
knows what the weather was that day.

`backfill_weather_for_shift` reads the archive API for one closed shift, at that
API's own **hourly** granularity. A live-swept shift carries a reading every
fifteen minutes and a backfilled one carries one an hour; that is a real difference
between contemporaneous evidence and reconstruction, and every row's `source`
column states which it is. `get_weather_timeline` calls out a **mixed** timeline by
name.

It is **idempotent to the minute** — not to the second, because the archive answers
on the hour and the live fetch answers with whatever the API rounded to, and
deduplicating on the minute is what makes a backfill over an also-swept shift keep
the live readings and fill only the gaps. A reading is never edited. Running it
twice appends nothing and reports what it skipped.

The archive answers by **whole days**, so readings outside the shift's own period
are dropped and counted. A six-hour morning shift must not acquire a timeline
running to midnight: that would be a defensible weather record and a false
statement about an exposure period.

**It writes no compliance events, and that is the one judgement call in the tool.**
A backfilled crossing is a true statement about the weather and a false one about
the shift — a `Threshold Crossed` row dated last July on a closed, signed record
would put an observation on the timeline that nobody made, next to water breaks
somebody did. The crossings are counted and reported instead, which is also what
makes the numbers usable: "four of these readings were over 80 °F" is the sentence
that tells a foreman whether this shift needed a heat record.

`list_shifts_missing_weather` is the worklist. The test is **one reading per hour**
of shift — the archive's own granularity, so a fully backfilled shift never appears
and a live-swept one clears it four times over. It is a heuristic and says so.
Shifts with **no `farm_location_gps`** are reported separately, because no amount
of backfilling documents one and the fix is a different action.

## 8. Five tools

Two mutating (default **OFF**), three read (default **on**).

| Tool | |
| --- | --- |
| `fetch_weather_now` | MUTATING. One reading on an open shift right now, bypassing the cache. Refuses a closed shift and names the tool to use instead. |
| `backfill_weather_for_shift` | MUTATING. Section 7. |
| `list_shifts_missing_weather` | Read. The backfill worklist. |
| `get_weather_timeline` | Read. One shift's conditions, windowable, with the extremes and `first_crossing`. |
| `get_weather_settings` | Read. The configuration and the active overrides. Answers even when weather is switched **off**, because it is the tool somebody calls to find out why nothing is being fetched. |

### There is no `update_weather_settings`

Weather Settings holds three outbound URLs and three compliance thresholds. A tool
that could write them would be a tool that could point this site's weather at a
server of somebody else's choosing, or raise the heat threshold to 200 °F so that
no shift ever crosses it — and either of those is a change an operator would never
find, because the resulting site behaves normally and simply never says anything is
wrong. Both are one sentence away from any model that can call it.

The configuration surface is the Desk form, where a human types the number and
Frappe's own version trail records who typed it. `get_weather_settings` reads it
back, which is the half that helps and carries none of the risk.

The guards are the shift tools', imported rather than restated: `require_hr_role`
(System Manager, HR Manager, HR User or Farm Manager), `require_company_scope`, the
`Weather Settings.enabled` kill switch, an MCP Action Log row per call, and both
mutating tools off by default. A weather reading is not personnel data, but the
thing it is appended to is: these tools write to a Farm Shift, which names who was
at work and for how long.

## 9. Judgement calls, stated

- **Precipitation is requested in millimetres, not inches.** The column is
  `precipitation_mm` and says mm on the form. A unit that disagrees with its own
  label is the kind of error that survives for years and is discovered by somebody
  comparing a rain figure against a gauge. Temperature and wind *are* converted,
  because those columns say °F and mph.
- **`current=` rather than `minutely_15=`.** The sweep's cadence *is* fifteen
  minutes, so the current block is the reading at the moment of asking; a
  fifteen-minute series would return that same instant plus a run of forecasts this
  app does not store. The parameter is there when a finer live cadence is ever
  wanted; asking for it today would be requesting data to throw away.
- **No per-shift threshold override.** A threshold is a policy about an operation,
  not a property of one afternoon. A shift that could carry its own would be a
  shift whose foreman could raise the bar on the hot day, which is precisely the
  day the number exists for. Company overrides only.
- **Readings are deduplicated on the minute, not the second.** See section 7.
- **The cache is per worker process.** A cold cache costs one extra request per
  place; a shared one is a new failure mode in the path whose job is not to have
  any.

## 11. What this sets up

`Farm Shift Weather Reading` becomes a valid CCF trigger source in v0.22.5, where
a Weather Threshold rule is a **record** rather than code: `target_source =
"Shift Weather Timeline"`, `trigger_expression = "temp_f >= 80 OR heat_index_f >=
80"`, `producer_task_template = "Water Break Documentation"`,
`producer_task_assign_to = shift.foreman`, `regulation_citation = "OAR
437-004-1131"`. When Oregon moves the threshold, an operator edits a row.

The thresholds on Weather Settings are the interim form of that, and they are
deliberately fields rather than constants for the same reason. The hardcoded
evaluation in `services/weather.py` is what CCF will replace; the fetch, the cache,
the backoff and the immutable append are what it will run on.
