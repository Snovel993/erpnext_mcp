# v0.38.0 — the rule was easy to write and nobody was watching the regulation

**v0.22.0 made a compliance rule a record. v0.37.0 let a model draft one from
regulation text and a person approve it. Neither of them has anything to say six
months later, when OR-OSHA renumbers a subsection, the ODA reissues its GAP
checklist, or a certifier publishes a handbook with a new water-testing interval
in it. The rules got easy to write and stayed exactly as hard to keep current,
which is the half that decays quietly.**

Seven new MCP tools (339 total: 156 read, 183 mutating). Two new DocTypes —
`Regulation Feed` and its one-column child table. One new service,
`erpnext_mcp/services/regulation_feed.py`. One new scheduled job, the seventh.

---

## The thing to be clear about first: it detects, and it does not remediate

It would be easy to read "regulation change detection" as *the app now keeps the
rules current by itself*. It does not, and the refusal is structural rather than
a feature nobody got round to.

A changed page is evidence that **somebody should read a regulation again.** It
is not evidence about what the regulation now says, and it is not authority to
rewrite a rule that is firing on somebody's compliance calendar.

The failure mode of the other design is specific and bad, and it is worth writing
out because it is the design a reasonable person would propose in the next
planning meeting: a rulebook page reflows for a redesign; a scraper reads the
shade threshold out of the new layout and gets it wrong by two degrees; a rule is
silently updated at four in the morning; and an operation spends a season
inspecting to a number nobody chose, with an audit trail saying the change was
automatic. Every step of that is plausible and none of it is visible.

So a detected change writes four things — a hash, a timestamp, a log line, and
the `rule_id` of every rule derived from that source — **and stops.**

What happens next is a person reading the source, and, where a rule genuinely
needs to change, the path v0.37.0 built and this release does not go round:

* `propose_compliance_rule` drafts the replacement, DISABLED, marked
  `AI-proposed`, with its citation on the record;
* `approve_compliance_rule` supersedes the live rule, with somebody's name on it.

`tests_standalone/test_regulation_feeds.py::ItDetectsAndItDoesNotRemediate`
asserts the rule row is identical after a detected change, and after a sweep that
detected one. That test is the guarantee; this section is the argument for it.

## Why the hash is of normalised text and not of the bytes

**Because a detector that always fires detects nothing.**

A state rulebook page carries a "Last updated 08/05/2026 14:02" stamp in its
footer, a CSRF nonce in a hidden input, a build fingerprint on its stylesheet
link, and a copyright year. A hash of the bytes reports a change on every single
check, for ever. The first week somebody reads those alerts. The second week
nobody does. The month the rule actually changes, the alert is indistinguishable
from the noise it has been buried in since the day it was switched on.

`normalise` throws away, in this order: `<script>`, `<style>` and `<noscript>`
blocks whole; HTML comments; every tag; then entity escapes (after the tags, so
`&lt;section&gt;` in the body stays the text the page was quoting); then ISO, US
and month-name dates, clock times with or without a meridiem, and hex strings of
sixteen characters or more; then every whitespace difference. What is left is the
readable prose, and a hash of that changes when the prose changes.

**The cost is real and it is stated rather than hidden.** A change that is *only*
a date is invisible to this detector — a compliance deadline moved from March 1
to April 1, with no other edit anywhere on the page, does not move the hash.
There is a test asserting exactly that, so a release that changes its mind about
the trade has to change it on purpose rather than by accident.

The mitigation is the `description` field. A feed whose whole content is a date
is a feed to describe as such and to read on its own schedule, rather than one to
trust a hash about.

One thing deliberately survives normalisation: **a citation number.** `437-004-1131`
looks like a date to a careless regex, and a rulebook whose every citation was
redacted would normalise two different divisions to the same string. The US-date
pattern is anchored so it cannot match.

## `Regulation Feed`, and the four fields nobody may type

The record is a pointer: `url`, `regime`, `company`, `description`,
`check_frequency`, `status`, and `affected_rules` — the Compliance Rules that
were written from this source.

Four more fields are the detector's own memory, and they are written only by a
check:

| Field | What it means |
|---|---|
| `last_content_hash` | SHA-256 of the normalised text at the last **successful** check |
| `last_checked` | when the source was last successfully asked |
| `last_change_detected` | when the content last differed. What `list_regulation_changes` filters on |
| `change_log` | append-only, one timestamped line per change, error and recovery |

**All four are REFUSED as arguments to `update_regulation_feed`** rather than
ignored. A hash somebody typed is a change that will never be reported, and a
change log somebody edited is the one record here whose entire value is that
nobody edited it.

The log is oldest-first on the record and newest-first when read, and at its cap
the **oldest** lines are dropped with a line saying so. Dropping the newest would
be a detector that filled up and quietly switched itself off.

`affected_rules` points one way and one way only. A change names those rules in
the log so a reader knows where to look; nothing in this app edits, disables or
supersedes a rule because a web page changed.

## Four outcomes, and each of them lands on the record

**BASELINE.** A feed with no stored hash. A first check cannot be a change,
because there is nothing to have changed from — so the hash is recorded and
`last_change_detected` is deliberately left empty. A feed registered today has
not moved, and saying it has would put it at the top of every "what changed" list
on the site.

**UNCHANGED.** `last_checked` moves and nothing else does, and **no log line is
written.** A weekly feed accumulating a hundred and fifty "still the same" lines
a year is a log the three real changes are buried in.

**CHANGED.** Both hashes, the size of the normalised text, and the rules to
re-read.

**ERROR.** Status and message on the record, a log line, and **`last_checked`
deliberately not moved** — so a monthly feed that failed today is retried tomorrow
rather than in thirty days.

A check that succeeds after an error clears the status back to Active and writes
a RECOVERED line, because a source unreachable for an afternoon must not become a
source nobody ever checks again.

## Paused is the kill switch. Error is a report.

`Error` is what the last check said, not a decision anybody made: the sweep
retries an errored feed, and `_status_or_refuse` refuses to let a tool set `Error`
by hand, because a feed marked Error with nothing wrong is a register that lies
about itself.

`Paused` is the decision. It is the only state that keeps a feed out of the
sweep, and it keeps the whole change log.

**There is no `delete_regulation_feed`**, for the same reason there is no delete
on a Compliance Rule. A source this operation watched for two seasons is a record
of what it was watching and when — which is exactly the question an auditor asks
about a rule whose citation has been renumbered twice. Deleting the row would
take the log with it and leave nothing behind saying the source had ever been
watched.

## The seventh scheduled job

`erpnext_mcp.services.regulation_feed.sweep_due_feeds`, at `0 4 * * *`.

It is the **second** job in this app that talks to somebody else's server — the
weather sweep was the first — and the first that talks to a *different* server per
record: a state agency's rulebook host, a certifier's WordPress.

It clears the same two bars every job on this schedule clears. It writes only
this app's own `Regulation Feed`, and it never raises. One agency site behind a
WAF does not stop the other eleven being checked, and a feed whose check throws
lands as an error on its own row while the sweep carries on.

Each feed's `check_frequency` is the **floor** and the cron is the **ceiling**,
exactly as `Weather Settings.fetch_interval_minutes` is: a Frappe cron expression
is a static string in `hooks.py` and cannot be rewritten from a form, so the
sweep cannot run more often than it is scheduled, and a Monthly feed is skipped
on the twenty-nine mornings it is not due. The one-hour tolerance on the due
calculation is why a Daily feed checked at 03:00:07 yesterday is due at 03:00:02
today: without it a fixed-time cron skips a day in two runs out of three, and a
"daily" feed is checked every other day — invisible until somebody counts the log
lines a year later.

It is **safe to run at any cadence**, for two independent reasons. The due check
skips a feed looked at recently, which is also the courtesy owed to a public
server nobody is paying for. And every run is a full comparison against stored
state rather than an increment, so a second run in the same hour finds the hash
the first one wrote and reports no change.

`check_all_regulation_feeds` calls the same function with the same due logic. A
manual sweep with a second implementation is a manual sweep that can disagree
with the nightly one.

## The seven tools

| Tool | Ships | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| `list_regulation_feeds` | on | The register, with status, frequency and what has never been checked |
| `get_regulation_feed` | on | One source in full, including its change log |
| `list_regulation_changes` | on | **What moved since our last compliance review** |
| `create_regulation_feed` | off | Register a source. Fetches nothing |
| `update_regulation_feed` | off | Edit one; pause one. Cannot write the detector's memory |
| `check_regulation_feed` | off | Fetch one source now |
| `check_all_regulation_feeds` | off | The sweep, on demand |

**Three reads ship on and four writes ship off.** The register is readable out of
the box; nothing reaches out of the box. Two of the four writes make outbound
requests to servers this operation does not own, which is a decision an operator
makes rather than inherits — and until the switch is ticked, nothing on the site
talks to anybody at all.

`list_regulation_changes` is the one the release exists for. It is a filter on
`last_change_detected` and nothing cleverer, which is the point: the fact was
recorded when it happened, by the sweep, so answering *what regulations moved
since our last review* later costs one query and no network at all. Its
`rules_to_review` is a **reading list, not a changelog of your compliance
calendar** — every rule named there is still running on exactly the definition a
person approved.

## Registering a source well

Point a feed at the **narrowest page that carries the rule**: a division of the
rulebook rather than the rulebook's index, a specific Federal Register document
rather than the search results that found it. A broad page changes for reasons
that have nothing to do with this operation, and every one of those changes is a
person asked to read a regulation for nothing — which is how a register stops
being read.

Name it after the regulation rather than after the website. "OAR 437-004
Agricultural Labor" is a source anybody on the operation can place; "OSHA page"
is a row nobody can tell from the other three.

Write a description somebody can act on. It is what a person reads when the log
says this source moved, and it is the difference between *go and read a rulebook*
and *go and check whether the shade requirement changed*. A description shorter
than a sentence is refused.

## Tests

`tests_standalone/test_regulation_feeds.py` — 89 tests over eight claims, with
every HTTP fetch mocked by a module installed into `sys.modules`, so the real
`import requests` inside `fetch` is exercised rather than patched around.

The two worth reading are `NormalisingIsWhatMakesTheDetectorUsable` — including
the test that asserts the *cost*, that a date-only change is invisible — and
`ItDetectsAndItDoesNotRemediate`, which is the release's whole promise written as
assertions about a rule row that did not move.
