# v0.35.0 — the hours were already written down

**v0.30.0 built a payroll engine. v0.19.3 built a shift register. The join
between them returned the crew's span for everybody, zero overtime, and zero
buckets.**

Three new MCP tools (329 total: 153 read, 176 mutating). No new DocTypes. One
new pure module, `payroll_integration.py`, on the same contract as
`payroll_calc.py` and `form_generators.py`. One real bug fixed in a released
tool.

---

## What was actually broken

`tools/payroll.py::_load_shifts` was forty lines long and looked finished. It
found the shifts an employee's crew rows pointed at, computed
`end_datetime - start_datetime`, and returned:

```python
{
    "work_state": shift.get("work_state", ""),
    "hours": round(hours, 2),
    "overtime_hours": 0.0,
    "piece_units": 0.0,
    "break_hours": 0.0,
}
```

Three of those five are hardcoded zeros, and the fourth is wrong. The hours are
the **crew's** span, not the worker's, so somebody who joined at seven and left
at one was paid for the whole nine-hour day. Overtime was never computed at all,
by anybody, anywhere in the payroll path — so a forty-five-hour week produced
forty-five straight hours and the employer under-paid five hours of premium.
Piece units were zero, so a piece-rate worker's gross pay was zero unless
somebody typed a number in.

There was also a genuine bug underneath it:

```python
filters={
    "name": ("in", shift_names),
    "start_datetime": (">=", start),
    "start_datetime": ("<=", f"{end} 23:59:59"),   # <- the first one is gone
}
```

Two entries with the same key in a Python dict literal is one entry. The
lower bound was discarded at parse time, so every shift in the register from the
beginning of time up to the period end was in scope. It never showed up because
the hours it returned were being retyped by hand anyway.

`tools/taxforms.py` had a comment pointing at this bug. The comment now points
at the fix.

## The unit of work is a segment

A shift is crew-shaped. Payroll is person-shaped. `shifts.py` resolved that
tension in v0.19.3 and wrote it down at length: the crew table is not a list of
names, every row carries its own `joined_at` and `left_at`, and
`remove_worker_from_shift` sets the second rather than deleting the row.

So the thing this release aggregates is a **segment** — one person's own stretch
of one shift. "The crew worked 06:00 to 15:00" and "Ana joined at 07:10 and left
at 13:00" are both true, both stored, and neither derived from the other. The
one payroll reads is Ana's.

A crew row with no `joined_at` inherits the shift's start and one with no
`left_at` inherits its end, because that is what the close writes for somebody
who worked the whole day.

## Overtime, weekly and in time order

Oregon HB 4002 and Washington SB 5172 both put agricultural overtime at **40
hours in a workweek**, both fully phased as of 2025, both at 1.5x.

A pay period is not a workweek. A biweekly period is two of them, and this is
the case that decides the design:

| | hours | overtime |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Week 1 | 45 | **5** |
| Week 2 | 35 | 0 |
| Period total | 80 | **5**, not 0 |

Summing the period and comparing to eighty finds none of it. That is the
arithmetic a hand-keyed payroll does, and it is the arithmetic a wage claim
undoes.

Segments are bucketed into seven-day weeks anchored at `pay_period_start`, with
`workweek_anchor` available for an employer whose declared workweek does not
line up with the period. Each week is then walked **in time order**, and the
hours past the fortieth are the overtime.

### Chronological, because it also decides the state

Proportional allocation would have been simpler and would have produced a number
about a day that did not happen. A picker who works Monday to Thursday in Oregon
at ten hours a day has forty hours before Friday starts, so a Washington Friday
is eight hours of overtime — **Washington's** overtime, at Washington's rate, in
Washington's tax allocation.

```
overtime_hours_by_state == {"OR": 0.0, "WA": 8.0}
```

## Two kinds of break, and they are not the same kind

| | in `total_hours`? | counts toward 40? | paid? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `break_hours` — paid rest | **yes** | yes | yes, at the average hourly |
| `unpaid_break_hours` — meal | no, subtracted | no | no |

Paid rest stays on the clock and is handed to the engine separately so the
piece-rate path can pay it at the average the period actually earned — WAC
296-131-020, and OAR 839-020-0050 to the same effect. Three hundred buckets in
seven and a half picking hours is $40 an hour, so a half-hour rest break is
worth $20 and the slip says so.

The meal period comes off the span. Five nine-hour shifts with a half-hour lunch
each is 42.5 hours, which is 2.5 hours of overtime rather than five.

Conflating the two is the standard way a farm ends up owing back wages, so they
are two fields and never one.

## Minimum wage, per state, reported and not remedied

Washington's floor is $16.66 and Oregon's is $14.70. A single check against the
period total would let a compliant Washington week paper over an Oregon week
that was not:

```
OR: 10 hours, $50 earned   → $5.00/hr   BELOW
WA: 30 hours, $1,000       → $33.33/hr  fine
combined:  40 hours, $1,050 → $26.25/hr  "fine"
```

So the check runs state by state, against that state's own floor and its own
region — Portland metro's $15.95 is an argument, not a branch — and prices the
shortfall.

**And stops.** `total_shortfall` says what it would cost to bring those hours up
to the floor; nothing adds it to gross. Whether the top-up is paid this period,
fixed on the piece rate, or disputed is a decision with a person's name on it,
and a payroll engine that quietly inflated gross would hide the fact that a rate
is set too low to be lawful.

## Where the piece units come from is a property of the site

Neither candidate source belongs to this app. The BucketLog bridge is written by
whichever version of the iPad app is in the field this season; Farm Task
Assignment records duration, not output, unless a site has added a column. So
all of them are inspected rather than assumed:

* **Bucket Log Entry** — the picker column, the date column and the count column
  are each found by name-candidate search. A row with **no count column is one
  bucket**, because the row is the record of the bucket, and reading it as zero
  would pay somebody nothing for a day of picking.
* **Farm Task Assignment** — used where the site has added a count column.
* **The crew row itself** — used where the site has added one there, which is
  the right place to record it.

They are **summed, not preferred**. A farm that changes how it records buckets
mid-season should be paid for both halves, not for whichever the code checked
first.

Every source that is absent is **named in the result**:

> The `Bucket Log Entry` doctype (the BucketLog bridge) is not on this site, so
> no bucket counts were read.

A piece-rate run that found nothing has produced a set of zeros, and whether
that means nobody picked or means the bridge is not installed is the whole
difference between a payroll and a mistake.

### Buckets with no shift behind them

Matched to a shift by `(employee, date)`, because neither source carries a link.
What matches nothing is **still paid** — the buckets are in the barn either
way — as a zero-hour stand-in that carries the units, takes its work state from
where that worker spent their other hours in the period, and is counted in
`piece_rows_without_a_shift`. The money is owed and the time was never recorded,
and both facts survive onto the slip.

## The three tools

### `get_employee_timesheet_summary`

**READ (default ON).** One employee's hours in a date range: their own spans,
the weekly overtime breakdown, the state split, breaks separated by kind, piece
units, and every shift that fed the total.

**The hours without the money, and that is why it is its own tool.** "Why is my
cheque this?" is answered by the timesheet, not the withholding. A foreman who
can show somebody their own five nines and the five hours of premium that came
out of them has answered the question before it becomes a dispute — and doing
that should not require the switches that let a caller see everybody's pay.

| Parameter | Required | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `employee` | yes | Docname or employee name |
| `start_date` | yes | `YYYY-MM-DD` (`pay_period_start` is an alias) |
| `end_date` | yes | `YYYY-MM-DD` (`pay_period_end` is an alias) |
| `company` | | Scopes the shift read |
| `overtime_threshold` | | Default 40 |
| `workweek_anchor` | | First day of the declared workweek |

### `preview_payroll_for_period`

**READ (default ON).** A whole company's period, computed and not written. Same
arithmetic and the same code path as the run — `_period_run` is one function
precisely so a preview that disagreed with what it previews is not possible.

Three lists to read before the totals: `employees_missing_structures`,
`totals.below_minimum_wage`, `totals.with_open_shifts`.

`detail=1` adds the per-shift timesheet, the state tax breakdown and the federal
working behind each slip. It is large, which is why it is opt-in.

### `run_payroll_for_period`

**MUTATING (default OFF).** The same calculation, stored as a Farm Payroll Entry
in **Calculated** status with one slip per employee.

Calculated and never Submitted: `submit_payroll` is a separate tool behind a
separate switch, because arithmetic anybody can redo and a statement that this
is what the farm is paying are two different acts.

**It does not refuse a run with problems in it.** A worker below minimum wage, a
shift nobody ended, a picker with no salary structure — reported, all of them,
and none of them a reason to hold up the pay of everybody else on the crew. Same
posture `end_shift` takes towards a shift with an unmet obligation: state it,
keep it.

The one refusal is a run where **nobody** can be paid, and it names them:

> Workers were on shift but none of them has an active salary structure — Beto
> Cruz. `create_salary_structure` is what makes their hours payable.

## What the v0.30.0 tools got out of this

`preview_payroll` and `calculate_payroll` were rewired rather than left behind.
Both read through the same aggregation now, so the single-employee preview
reports the same 45 hours, the same 5 of overtime and the same $950 as the
company-wide run does for the same person. A site that never turns the new
switches on still stops paying the crew's span.

Year-to-date also carries now: the Social Security wage base is an annual
per-person cap, and a period run that could not see the periods before it would
restart it and over-withhold on anybody who had crossed $176,100. Prior
`Calculated` and `Submitted` entries in the same calendar year are read first —
the same status rule `tools/taxforms.py` applies, for the same reason.

## What this release deliberately did not change

**The overtime premium for piece work.** `payroll_calc` pays the full 1.5x of
the effective hourly *on top of* piece earnings that already cover straight time
for those hours; the strict FLSA method is a 0.5x premium on top. The difference
is in the worker's favour. Changing what a released engine pays is not a thing
to do inside an integration release, and erring high on somebody's overtime is
the safe direction to err in. It is written down in `payroll_integration.py` so
the next person to read it knows it was a decision.

**Makeup pay.** Reported, priced, never applied. See above.

**Anything about the shift tools.** No new field on Farm Shift Crew Member, no
`piece_units` argument on `add_worker_to_shift`. A site that adds the column has
it read; adding it here would have meant a schema change and a tool-surface
change inside a release whose whole job was to connect two things that already
existed.
